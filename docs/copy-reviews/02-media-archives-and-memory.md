A digital photograph has more than one life. There is the moment it records. Then there are the decisions that determine its future: which files survive, how they are arranged, what an interface lets the viewer do, and whether other people can add to the image.

Three related projects make those decisions visible. *640 × 480* is a long-running photographic archive and time-based work. Pixilation supplies a player for viewing its sequences. *Narcissus As Narcosis* is a separate participatory project whose Mashup application now has a web presence at Picarty. The projects share an interest in images as things people use, while their sources and forms remain distinct.

## 640 × 480: the sequence is the work

Goldberg began making *640 × 480* images in Shanghai in 2002, using a small early digital camera. Its frames measured 640 by 480 pixels. At the time, an image that size could occupy much of a computer screen. The limits of the camera remain legible in the archive: noise, unstable colour and a small field of detail.

Looking at the files in quick succession revealed another possibility. Still frames began to behave like movement. Goldberg started photographing gestures, walks and changing rooms with the next frame in mind. An individual image could be held, but a sequence could carry time.

The full body of photographs, the chronological albums, any later selected sequence and a single frame are different ways of encountering the material. Selection makes a work legible; it also changes what the archive appears to remember. The record includes gaps. It cannot show an event the camera did not capture or an image that did not survive.

This is why *640 × 480* needs more than a gallery of attractive stills. A frame can serve as evidence of a moment. Sequence gives that moment duration. The accumulated archive shows what repeated acts of looking leave behind.

## Pixilation: the viewer sets the time

At [Pixilation.org, the 640 × 480 player](https://pixilation.org/), the viewer can move through images instead of scanning thumbnails. Pace changes the reading. Rapid playback draws out rhythm and recurrence. Slower playback restores the weight of a particular face, street or gesture. Direction changes the apparent course of an action.

The viewer can make a temporary edit by moving through the material, but cannot make missing files reappear. The interface gives the archive a form without turning the historical record into a complete account of the past. Its controls are part of how the work is experienced.

## Picarty: the image becomes a shared decision

*Narcissus As Narcosis* asks a different question: who acts on an image, and who is allowed to call the result a work? The earlier Mashup application and the current [Picarty/Mashup web interface](https://picarty.com/) belong to this larger project, but they are distinct versions.

A participation session has several layers: a source capture, a participant's crop or mask, a generated composite, and any later selection by the artist. Those layers should remain visible when an image is described or exhibited. The person who supplies or changes an image, the software that combines it, and the artist who selects an outcome do different things. Their roles cannot be collapsed into one signature.

Picarty also changes the position of the viewer. With *640 × 480*, a visitor navigates an existing record. With Mashup, a participant helps produce an image that did not exist before the encounter. Both projects make the interface consequential. They do so through different kinds of agency.

Goldberg's [essay on Marshall McLuhan and the medium](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/writing/medium-is-the-message/) sets out a wider theory of how a medium shapes perception. The projects here provide particular cases: the low-resolution camera, the sequential player and the participatory composite. Each places a different decision between the world and the picture.
