Jared Goldberg's art uses more than objects. It uses names, reproductions, storefronts, prices, interfaces and the expectation that an artist will explain what any of these are worth. Some works invite play. Others make the transaction itself uncomfortable. The frame around an artwork can carry as much of the argument as the object inside it.

[Duchamped](https://duchamped.com/) is the principal home for the art practice. This page is a route into its questions and related projects. Artwork records, available editions and the work's own promotional language belong at their source.

## To Duchamp: changing the frame

Goldberg uses “to Duchamp” as a verb for taking a familiar thing and making it operate inside another frame. The thing may be an image, a product, a name or a market convention. Its source can remain recognizable while its use and implied value change.

The verb is Goldberg's artistic usage, explained in the [Duchamped manifesto](https://duchamped.com/duchamped/). It is useful because it names an operation rather than a style. A reproduced image, an inflated object and a staged investment page do not have to look alike to ask who is allowed to assign meaning and price.

That question has limits. Appropriation has a source, an owner and consequences. The relation between an artwork and the thing it borrows must be examined case by case; the verb does not settle it.

## The artist's names

Goldberg has worked under more than one public identity. *The Subversive Artist* appears as a persona in the current art presentation. *Jared the Jew* is a historical stage name and part of the work's record. Its use draws on Goldberg's account of encountering antisemitic language and taking those words into an artistic identity.

Reclaiming a name does not empty it of its prior force. Different audiences will encounter it with different histories. The name belongs in the archive with its context, while Jared Goldberg remains the name under which the wider practice can be found.

## The Pitch: when the offer is part of the work

*[The Pitch](https://duchamped.com/the-pitch/)* adopts the surface of an art-investment offer: an invitation, asset language, shares, price and claims of return. In this setting, the sales page is a performance. It asks how a number begins to feel credible when it is wrapped in the familiar signs of scarcity, expertise and financial opportunity.

The stated prices and returns are claims made within the work. They are not evidence here of independently verified market value, investment performance or an operating securities platform. The distinction matters because a staged offer can be read as an actual one.

Putting a Jewish stage name beside the language of finance can also evoke an antisemitic trope. The work may expose that association or be misread through it; no curatorial statement can decide the viewer's response in advance. The risk is part of how *The Pitch* circulates and must be named with care.

## Work, document, edition

An artwork, a photograph documenting it, a software output and something offered for sale can look similar on a screen. They have different histories and different claims to authorship. Duchamped should hold the artwork record; a retail imprint such as Pure Kitsch can hold editions or merchandise. The institutional index can show how those manifestations relate without treating a product listing as the entire work.

This distinction also leaves room for art that does not fit *The Pitch*. Photographic sequences, participatory composites, objects and proposed large-scale works may share an interest in circulation while asking different questions. The practice grows clearer when those differences remain visible.
