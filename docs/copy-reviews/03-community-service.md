An opportunity is shaped by its conditions. A young person can be told what a margin is, but learns more when a price has to meet a cost and another person's choice. A worker may be able to do a task, but be excluded by the hours, transport or noise attached to the job.

The Money Club and Capability Works approach different problems through the design of those conditions. Their statuses matter: one has run a first youth-program pilot; the other remains a proposed employment and housing model.

## The Money Club: learning inside a small economy

[The Money Club](https://the-money-club.org/) teaches financial literacy through making, pricing, communication and testing. In August 2026, its first week-long program ran at the UTSU Student Commons in Toronto. Students researched needs, developed offers, examined costs and brought the offers to a Maker Market.

The market used virtual cash. This let students see peers make choices with a limited budget while keeping the financial stakes low. A purchase with virtual cash is stronger feedback than a compliment. It is still a classroom signal, and it does not establish what the same buyer would pay with their own money in another setting.

That distinction makes the pilot useful. The program can ask where students needed help, whether they understood the difference between revenue and profit, how they worked together, and what a later session should change. The first run tested a method of learning. A repeatable program will require documentation, new facilitators and further evidence.

Goldberg gives his own account in [The Money Club: Maiden Voyage](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/projects/the-money-club/maiden-voyage/). His [essay on a deployable education system](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/writing/the-money-club-as-a-deployable-education-system/) describes an ambition beyond one week in Toronto. This page records the difference between the completed pilot and that longer ambition.

## Capability Works: redesigning the job

[Capability Works](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/projects/capital-works/) is a proposal for matching people's capabilities and preferred working conditions to tasks with real demand. It begins with a basic observation: a job description bundles the useful task together with a schedule, setting, pace and social demands. Some of those conditions are essential. Others may be inherited habits of the organization.

The proposed model would examine tasks and conditions separately, then build work with the person who will do it. It also imagines a relationship between paid work and more stable housing. That relationship has not been demonstrated by an operating employment platform. It will require partners, worker participation, research and a pilot that follows what happens after a placement.

Goldberg's [essay on the design of work](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/writing/the-future-of-work-is-a-design-problem/) explains the problem from the worker's point of view. A later [essay on dignity and continuity](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/writing/dignity-is-a-systems-output/) asks what happens when a job can be performed but transport, scheduling and support fail to meet. Here the institutional question is concrete: who has the power and information to repair the connection?

## What joins these projects

One project gives young people a controlled way to test an offer. The other asks how adults might be given better terms for paid contribution. They differ in maturity, audience and risk. Both suggest that advice is limited when the environment gives someone no usable way to act.

For The Money Club, the next evidence is another iteration with clear observations of learning and participation. For Capability Works, it is a participatory design process and a small test of sustained, fairly paid work. In both cases, the system should learn from the people whose lives it organizes.
