A product on a shelf can look like one decision. It is usually the end of many: what a factory can make, what a buyer can stock, what a store can sell, what a customer notices and what the next report measures. The same product changes meaning as it crosses those thresholds.

Goldberg has worked on different sides of this chain: teaching and product development in Shanghai; private-label portfolios at Loblaw; retail media at Walmart; and in-store media systems at Canadian Tire. His [first-person work accounts](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/work/index.html) describe what he saw inside those organizations. This page compares the decision structures those accounts bring into view.

## The factory: what can be repeated?

In China, Goldberg taught design and worked at Bestway, a maker of toys and sporting goods. His account of an innovation lab joins research, product design, manufacturing and the materials used to sell a product. A design can succeed on paper and still fail at tooling, cost, production or presentation.

This makes a factory an interesting site of authorship. A designer proposes a form. Production gives it tolerances and a price. A retailer asks for differentiation. The product that reaches a buyer is the result of negotiation among these demands. Goldberg describes that movement in his [account of China and supply chains](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/work/china.html).

## The shelf: who controls comparison?

At Loblaw, the retailer did more than buy products. It could develop private-label offers, compare them with national brands and decide how the assortment would appear on its own shelves. A brand invests in being chosen; a retailer controls the setting in which the choice is made.

Internal decisions also have an audience. A proposal needs evidence, a financial case and people willing to act on it. In Goldberg's [Loblaw account](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/work/loblaw.html), the competition for investment is as important as the competition between products. Shelf space is physical; attention and approval inside the company are scarce resources too.

## The store: who sets the default?

Goldberg's [Walmart account](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/work/walmart.html) pays attention to repeated store patterns and operating rituals. Layout, price promises and routines can align many people without a new instruction for every choice. Scale depends partly on the ability to repeat a rule; it also makes exceptions harder to see.

At Canadian Tire, the dealer model introduces another form of control. Local operators have room to respond to their markets, while national banners and shared systems create a common network. An automotive errand, a sporting-goods purchase and a workwear trip each put a shopper in a different frame of mind. A media placement that works in one setting cannot simply be assumed to work in another. Goldberg discusses those conditions in his [Canadian Tire account](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/work/canadian-tire.html).

## The measure: what result counts?

Retail media joins physical space to advertising and measurement. A screen can be sold as an impression; a merchant still needs the right stock, price and store experience. A measured sale may include products that were never advertised. Counting what happened is different from showing what the media caused.

The question for an institution is therefore practical: whose objective is being optimized, who can change the conditions and what evidence would show an improvement for the shopper and the business? A dashboard can report an outcome while leaving the decision that produced it obscure.

Goldberg develops the incentive argument in [What Real Systems Taught Me About Incentives](https://jaredgoldberg.ca/writing/real-systems-incentives/). The cases gathered here add a comparative view. The factory, the shelf and the store have different owners and constraints. Each decides what becomes visible and what can be done next. These are accounts of Goldberg's work, not statements on behalf of the organizations named.
